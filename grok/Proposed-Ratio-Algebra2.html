Yes. There are several existing mathematical structures that come remarkably close to pieces of what you are describing, but I do not know of a standard algebra whose definition is exactly:

two dimensionally congruent but algebraically distinct/orthogonal 3-spaces, regarded as reciprocal numerator and denominator aspects, whose quotient is intentionally not evaluated into an element of the underlying scalar field.

I would not yet call the proposed object a new division algebra, because the word “division algebra” imposes quite strong algebraic conditions that your irreducible-ratio requirement may actually work against. 1. A couple of mathematical corrections that help sharpen your idea

First, Q is not actually a discrete number system in its usual topology. It is dense but incomplete.

Z⊂Q⊂R

and

∀a<b∈R,(a,b)∩Q

contains infinitely many points.

So the contrast you want is better expressed as:

countable + dense + incomplete​

versus

uncountable + complete​.

That is more precise than “discrete versus continuous.”

This becomes quite useful later, because structures such as

Q3,HQ​,Clp,q​(Q)

give you countable arithmetic structures that sit densely inside their corresponding real structures. Quaternion algebras over Q, for example, are perfectly standard objects. arXiv+1

Also, the fact that a rational m/n can be regarded as a real number does not arise because “the division has been carried out.” It is already an element of Q. There is simply an injective field embedding

Q↪R.

What is lost in passing from the formal expression m:n to the rational m/n is something different: the representation by numerator and denominator.

That is much closer to your concern.

2. Your S/T is probably not a scalar quotient

Suppose you genuinely have two distinct three-dimensional vector spaces

S≃K3,T≃K3,

where K might initially be R, Q, or something else.

If these are different types of objects, then mathematicians already have a natural way of writing something resembling

TS​

without identifying or cancelling them:

S⊗T∗​

where T∗ is the dual of T.

And

S⊗T∗≅Hom(T,S).

This is strikingly close to what you want.

It means:

an object which takes something of T-type and produces something of S-type.

If

dimS=dimT=3,

then

dim(S⊗T∗)=3×3=9.

So a genuinely three-dimensional “space divided by three-dimensional time” naturally produces a 9-component object, not an ordinary 3-vector and not a scalar.

In coordinates it is simply a 3×3 matrix:

V=​v11​v21​v31​​v12​v22​v32​​v13​v23​v33​​​.

But conceptually it is

V:T→S​.

That is a much more faithful mathematical analogue of an irreducible S:T than ordinary numerical division.
